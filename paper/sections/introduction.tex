\section{Introduction}
\setlength{\parskip}{1em}

Physics-based simulation of tsunami-like wave propagation can become expensive when repeated across many possible source scenarios, bathymetric fields, numerical resolutions, or uncertainty-analysis runs. This cost motivates surrogate models that approximate numerical rollouts more rapidly while retaining the spatial and temporal structure needed for scientific evaluation.

We cast forward emulation as an operator-learning problem: given a bathymetry field and an initial free-surface disturbance, the model predicts a future free-surface trajectory generated by a numerical reference solver. Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs) are a natural primary model because they learn mappings between discretized functions and can be evaluated on spatial fields. We also include a Factorized Fourier Neural Operator (F-FNO), mode-count ablations, U-FNO and WNO variants, while CNN, U-Net, and ConvLSTM models provide local, multiscale, and recurrent comparisons.

The numerical reference is part of the learning problem rather than a neutral source of labels. Two networks trained with the same inputs and architecture can learn different operators when their targets come from different discretizations, boundary treatments, or governing equations. This matters especially for multi-reference studies: equal tensor shapes do not imply equal physical meaning if frames are selected by solver-step index while adaptive step sizes differ. We therefore require every reference to publish its labels on one requested-time vector before comparing either solvers or surrogates.

Time alignment does not make one reference ground truth. Hydrostatic and MUSCL-HR approximate the depth-averaged shallow-water equations with different spatial and temporal discretizations, while the Boussinesq comparison introduces a linear weakly dispersive correction. Their pairwise gaps combine equation-set and numerical effects. We consequently report same-reference surrogate error, raw reference-to-reference discrepancy, and cross-reference surrogate discrepancy as separate quantities. This separation is central to the paper: a surrogate can move closer to a second numerical reference without becoming more accurate with respect to the real ocean.

The goal is not to replace site-specific tsunami models or to claim operational early-warning readiness. Instead, we ask a narrower question: after the numerical references are aligned to the same requested times, how much of the apparent surrogate result is due to the neural model, and how much is due to the reference solver that defines its labels? We then test how the answer changes under distribution shift, resolution transfer, predictive uncertainty, and transfer from synthetic to real bathymetric morphology.

\noindent
The main contributions of this study are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=3.6em]
    \item a common-time benchmark with 50 shared requested times, solver-specific natural time stepping, a buffered $96 \times 96$ computational domain, and a central $64 \times 64$ publication crop,
    \item a numerically assessed three-reference design with Hydrostatic as the primary reference, MUSCL-HR as a second shallow-water reference, and Boussinesq as an exploratory weakly dispersive reference on identical scenarios,
    \item eleven direct model--reference configurations comprising nine Hydrostatic architectures plus FNO models trained on MUSCL-HR and Boussinesq, together with seeded windowed models for conditional long-horizon diagnostics,
    \item an evidence suite covering common-time solver gaps, cross-reference discrepancy ratios, sample scaling, strict held-out families, native and proxy resolution transfer, gauge/arrival/peak diagnostics, a seven-member ensemble, and synthetic-forcing transfer to real bathymetric morphology.
\end{enumerate}
