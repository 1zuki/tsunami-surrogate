\section{Problem formulation and evaluation framework}
\label{sec:methods}

This section defines the task, references, and metrics; data, models, and training are detailed in Sections~\ref{sec:data-generation}--\ref{sec:training}.

\subsection{Forward emulation task}
\label{subsec:forward-task}
Let $\Omega = [0, 1]^2$ denote the normalized two-dimensional spatial domain. Each benchmark scenario is characterized by a bathymetric profile $b(x,y)$, a baseline source template $q(x,y)$, and an initial water depth $h_0(x,y)$. Under the convention where submerged regions satisfy $b(x,y) < 0$, the quiescent water depth field is defined as:
\begin{equation}
h_{\mathrm{rest}}(x, y) = \max\{-b(x, y) + \eta_{\mathrm{sea}}, 0\}.
\end{equation}
Given a sampled source-strength multiplier $s$, the initial free-surface perturbation $\eta_0(x,y)$ and total fluid thickness $h_0(x,y)$ are evaluated via:
\begin{equation}
\eta_0(x, y) = s\,q(x, y), \qquad h_0(x, y) = \max\{h_{\mathrm{rest}}(x, y) + \eta_0(x, y), 0\}.
\end{equation}
For dataset construction, the source-strength multiplier $s$ is sampled from $[0.15, 0.30]$ and may be reduced before storage when the initial-elevation-to-depth cap is active; consequently, some evaluated scenarios have resolved strengths below the sampling interval.
For the supervised surrogate modeling task, the default input representation concatenates these spatial fields into a three-channel tensor $X = [b, q, h_0]\in\mathbb{R} ^ {3\times H\times W}$.

Note that the input tensor incorporates the raw source profile $q$ instead of the scaled perturbation $\eta_0$; both quantities are stored with the scenario metadata. For a given numerical solver reference $r$, the target trajectory $Y_r$ consists of 50 discrete temporal snapshots defined on a $64 \times 64$ spatial grid:
\begin{equation}
Y_r = \left[\eta_r(t_1), \dots, \eta_r(t_{50})\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{50 \times 64 \times 64},
\end{equation}
sampled at the requested evaluation times $t_k = 8.4k$ ($k = 1, \dots, 50$).

The sequence excludes $t=0$ and ends at $420$ in benchmark-time units, without a site-specific dimensional interpretation.

For reproducibility, the normalized coordinates are tied to the benchmark grid by explicit metadata. The central $128 \times 128$ solver region spans $2400$ length units with $\Delta x = \Delta y = 18.75$; a 32-cell buffer on each side therefore gives a $192 \times 192$ computational grid spanning $3600$ length units, and the reported $64 \times 64$ target is the block-mean reduction of the central solver region. The metadata specify a horizontal scale of $2400$~m, a time scale of $2400$~s, a vertical scale of $1$~m, and aspect ratio $2400$. These values document the intended coordinate relationship; the solver and neural-network inputs remain in benchmark units, and no site-specific dimensional ocean interpretation is implied.

\subsection{Common-time extraction and buffered domain}
\label{subsec:common-time-extraction}

Each reference advances on its own natural time sequence $\tau_r ^ 0 < \tau_r ^ 1 < \cdots$. The shallow-water steps adapt to the evolving state, whereas the Boussinesq step is CFL-selected once from the fixed depth field of each scenario and remains constant within that rollout.
When a target time $t_k$ falls between two consecutive solver steps ($\tau_r^n < t_k < \tau_r^{n + 1}$), the interpolation weight $\lambda_{r,k} \in (0, 1)$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{r, k} = \frac{t_k - \tau_r ^ n}{\tau_r ^ {n + 1} - \tau_r ^ n}
\end{equation}
The corresponding output state is then linearly reconstructed as:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{Z}_r(t_k) = (1 - \lambda_{r, k}) Z_r^n + \lambda_{r, k} Z_r^{n+1}
\end{equation}

For the shallow-water references, $Z = [h, hu, hv] ^ \top$ and the free surface is then recovered using the time-independent bathymetry; for Boussinesq, $\eta$ is already part of the evolved state. If $t_k$ exactly equals a natural time, the corresponding state is copied without interpolation. Several requested times may fall inside one natural-step interval, but every output is formed only from that adjacent pair.

Interpolation uses float64 before reported frames are cast to float32. It never feeds back into integration. Metadata retain bracket times, weights, widths, exact-knot status, and natural-step indices. Common-time extraction removes solver-step-index sampling mismatch while retaining interpolation error and differences between governing models.

The $384 \times 384$ master input is averaged to $128 \times 128$, edge-extended by $32$ cells per side, and integrated on $192 \times 192$. Source tapering occurs on the master grid. The central solver region is averaged to $64 \times 64$ output. Buffering and the sponge reduce exposure without establishing non-reflecting boundaries.

The surrogate approximates the mapping $f_\theta:X \mapsto\widehat{Y}_r\approx Y_r$.
Hydrostatic is the primary reference, MUSCL-HR the second shallow-water reference, and Boussinesq an exploratory weakly dispersive comparison.

\subsection{Numerical references}
\label{subsec:numerical-reference-formulation}
The reference shallow-water dynamics govern the state vector $U = [\, h, \, hu, \, hv \,]^\top$ (with free-surface elevation $\eta = h + b$) via the conservation law:
\begin{equation}
\partial_t U + \partial_x F(U) + \partial_y G(U) = S(U, b),
\end{equation}
with flux functions $F(U), G(U)$ and bed-slope source $S(U, b)$ specified as:
\begin{equation}
F(U) =
\begin{bmatrix}
    hu \\[2pt]
    \dfrac{(hu) ^ 2}{h} + \dfrac{1}{2}gh ^ 2 \\[6pt]
    \dfrac{hu\, hv}{h}    
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
G(U) =
\begin{bmatrix}
    hv \\[2pt]
    \dfrac{hu\, hv}{h} \\[6pt]
    \dfrac{(hv) ^ 2}{h} + \dfrac{1}{2}gh ^ 2
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
S(U, b) =
\begin{bmatrix}
    0 \\
    -gh\, \partial_x b \\
    -gh\, \partial_y b
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Here, $h$ is the fluid depth and $(hu, hv)$ are the momentum components, defining velocities $(u, v) = (hu/h, hv/h)$ in wet cells. To prevent division by zero, depths below a dry tolerance are regularized and assigned zero momentum. Although the domain remains fully wet by design, these safeguards ensure that any loss of positivity triggers an explicit numerical error rather than undefined states.

All spatial coordinates, depths, elevations, velocities, and times in these equations are benchmark quantities rather than SI measurements for a particular ocean basin. The resolved numerical coefficients are $g = 9.81$, dry tolerance $10^{-6}$, and maximum safeguarded velocity $30$ in those benchmark units. The familiar numerical value of $g$ does not by itself define a dimensional length--time mapping.

Both shallow-water baselines adopt finite-volume updates utilizing Rusanov (Lax–Friedrichs) intercell fluxes:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{F}(U_L, U_R) = \frac{1}{2}\left(F(U_L) + F(U_R)\right) - \frac{1}{2} \alpha_x(U_R - U_L),
\\
\alpha_x = \max\!\left\{|u_L| + \sqrt{gh_L},\, |u_R| + \sqrt{gh_R}\right\},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
and similarly for $\mathcal{G}$ in the $y$-direction \citep{toro2009riemann,toro2026rusanov}. The numerical conservative update reads:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
U_{i,j}^{n + 1} = U_{i, j}^{n}
&- \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}
\left(\mathcal{F}_{i + 1/2, j} - \mathcal{F}_{i - 1/2, j}\right) \\
&- \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta y}
\left(\mathcal{G}_{i, j + 1/2} - \mathcal{G}_{i, j - 1/2}\right)
+ \Delta t\,S_{i, j}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The well-balanced source treatment is incorporated differently by the two schemes. The Rusanov dissipation uses the largest local gravity-wave characteristic speed crossing each face. It is deliberately simple and robust: the principal difference between the two shallow-water references comes from their reconstruction and time integration, not from changing the approximate Riemann solver.

To satisfy the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition, the adaptive time step is evaluated as:
\begin{equation}
\Delta t = c_r\left[\max\!\left\{\frac{\max(|u| + \sqrt{gh})}{\Delta x}, \frac{\max(|v| + \sqrt{gh})}{\Delta y} \right\} \right] ^ {-1},
\end{equation}
where the Courant number $c_r$ is prescribed as $c_r = 0.1125$ for the Hydrostatic solver and $c_r = 0.225$ for the MUSCL-HR scheme. The wave speeds and therefore $\Delta t$ are recomputed from every natural state. The final step crossing a requested output time is not shortened to land on that time; instead, the two natural states continue to bracket the request and the output-only interpolation of Section~\ref{subsec:common-time-extraction} is applied.

\paragraph{Hydrostatic reconstruction reference.} The primary shallow-water baseline employs cell-centered states without slope reconstruction, advancing solutions via a first-order explicit finite-volume update. Interface water depths are reconstructed following Audusse et al. \citep{audusse2004hydrostatic}:
\begin{equation}
z ^ \star = \max\{b_L, b_R\}, \qquad
h_L ^ \star = \max\{0, h_L + b_L - z ^ \star\}, \qquad
h_R ^ \star = \max\{0, h_R + b_R - z ^ \star\},
\end{equation}
ensuring positivity preservation and the exact lake-at-rest condition (C-property). Reconstructed momenta retain host-cell velocities, with interface-specific pressure corrections absorbing local bathymetric gradients. By directly coupling topographic sources with interface flux differences, this formulation avoids ad-hoc bed-slope approximations. As a well-balanced, first-order scheme, it provides a robust baseline for evaluating higher-order models and neural surrogates.

\paragraph{MUSCL-HR reference.}
The MUSCL-HR scheme independently reconstructs $h$, $\eta = h + b$, $u$, and $v$ along each spatial dimension. For a scalar cell-centered field $q$, interface values are reconstructed using the componentwise minmod limiter:
\begin{equation}
s_i = \operatorname{minmod}(q_i - q_{i - 1},\, q_{i + 1} - q_i),
\qquad
q_i ^ \pm = q_i \pm\frac{1}{2}s_i.
\end{equation}
which zeroes gradients at local extrema to enforce TVD stability, reverting locally to first-order accuracy near discontinuities. Reconstructed depths are constrained to be non-negative, face velocities are clamped to the prescribed maximum speed bound, and face bathymetry is consistently retrieved via $b ^ \pm = \eta ^ \pm - h ^ \pm$. Hydrostatic reconstruction and the Rusanov flux are subsequently applied to these face states \citep{clain2016secondorder,hou2015muscl}.

\paragraph{Topographic source discretization}. Each Euler stage incorporates the reconstructed bed-slope momentum source:
\begin{equation}
S_{hu} = -\frac{g}{2}(h_E + h_W)\frac{b_E - b_W}{\Delta x},
\qquad
S_{hv} = -\frac{g}{2}(h_N + h_S)\frac{b_N - b_S}{\Delta y},
\end{equation}
where subscripts $\{E, W, N, S\}$ denote limited face states reconstructed at cell boundaries. Applied before the Heun stage averaging, this source term operates alongside the hydrostatic interface corrections. At a lake at rest with all reconstructed interfaces wet, constant $\eta$ implies $b_E - b_W = -(h_E - h_W)$. The corrected cell-side momentum fluxes are $gh_E ^ 2/2$ and $gh_W ^ 2/2$, so their difference contributes $-g(h_E ^ 2 - h_W ^ 2) / (2\Delta x)$ to the momentum tendency, while the reconstructed source contributes the opposite term. The same cancellation holds in the transverse direction and is retained by the Heun average, up to floating-point roundoff. Their combined stationary behavior is checked for three benchmark bathymetries over 32 steps in Appendix~\ref{app:lake-at-rest}; this finite check does not establish balance for moving equilibria or arbitrary reconstructed states.

Time integration uses a two-stage Heun form. Denoting one explicit limited finite-volume Euler step by $\Phi_{\Delta t}(U)$, the cell update reads:
\begin{equation}
U ^ {(1)} = \Phi_{\Delta t}(U ^ n),\qquad
U ^ {n + 1} = \frac{1}{2} U ^ n +
\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{\Delta t}\!\left(U ^ {(1)}\right).
\end{equation}

Positivity and dry-momentum projections are verified after the stage updates and after the final average. Consequently, MUSCL-HR differs from the primary reference through both its piecewise-linear spatial reconstruction and two-stage temporal integration, rather than a simple CFL adjustment.

\paragraph{Weakly dispersive Boussinesq comparison.}
The Boussinesq comparison evolves surface elevation and its time derivative, $[\eta, \eta_t] ^ \top$, rather than depth and discharge. We define it as a study-specific linear, elevation-only dispersive benchmark rather than a complete coastal Boussinesq model. Its variable-depth elliptic acceleration system is:
\begin{equation}
\left(I - \alpha_B \nabla \cdot(H ^ 2 \nabla)\right) a_\eta = g \nabla \cdot(H \nabla \eta),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
H(x, y) = \max\!\left\{(-b(x, y) + \eta_{\mathrm{sea}}) s_H, H_{\min}\right\}.
\end{equation}
We use $\alpha_B = 1/3$, $s_H = 1$, and $H_{\min} = 10 ^ {-4}$. Spatial gradients and variable-coefficient divergences are represented by centered second-order finite differences with depth coefficients averaged to cell faces. For fixed bathymetry, the dispersive mass operator on the left-hand side is unchanged throughout a rollout. Its face coefficients and sparse factorization are therefore cached, and each matrix-free conjugate-gradient acceleration solve uses the cached sparse-LU factor as a preconditioner. The relative residual tolerance is $10 ^ {-10}$ with at most $750$ iterations per solve.

Two acceleration solves form one velocity-Verlet step:
\begin{equation}
\eta ^ {n + 1} = \eta ^ n + \Delta t\, \eta_t ^ n + \frac{1}{2}\Delta t ^ 2a_\eta ^ n,
\qquad
\eta_t ^ {n + 1} = \eta_t ^ n + \frac{\Delta t}{2} \left(a_\eta ^ n + a_\eta ^ {n + 1} \right).
\end{equation}
Samples failing residual tolerance in either acceleration solve are discarded. For a fixed bathymetry, the time step is determined by the maximum long-wave speed:
\begin{equation}
\Delta t = \frac{0.35}{\sqrt{g\max(H)}\left(\Delta x ^ {-1} + \Delta y ^ {-1}\right)}.
\end{equation}
Designed as a linear, elevation-only benchmark, this weakly dispersive formulation provides a controlled equation-family contrast without serving as a full nonlinear coastal Boussinesq model or implying superior physical accuracy over the shallow-water references.

\paragraph{Boundaries, damping, and shared scope.}
The shallow-water references use bathymetry-aware linearized radiation states at all four outer edges. The outgoing free-surface characteristic is continued through the face around the local rest depth, while the tangential velocity is carried from the adjacent interior cell. This treatment reduces direct reflection for small outgoing waves but is not an exact non-reflecting condition for nonlinear, oblique, or strongly heterogeneous propagation. The Boussinesq comparison instead uses open zero-gradient scalar boundaries.

All references apply a 32-cell cosine sponge layer within the outer domain boundary. Given reference mask $D(x, y) \in [0.8, 1]$ and baseline step $\Delta t_{\mathrm{ref}} = 8.4$, per-step damping follows $D^{\Delta t/\Delta t_{\mathrm{ref}}}$, so that the mask factors multiply to the same attenuation factor for equal elapsed benchmark times. The resulting mass and energy changes still depend on the evolving state and the ordering of damping and dynamical updates. The shallow-water sponge damps momentum and free-surface displacement about rest depth; the Boussinesq sponge damps $\eta$ and $\eta_t$. The optional Boussinesq smoothing filter is disabled. Across all three references, the benchmark omits Coriolis acceleration, bottom friction, moving bathymetry, and event-specific forcing after initialization.

\subsection{Numerical accuracy and validation scope}
\label{subsec:reference-validation}

The evidence combines full-horizon and nested-grid sensitivities, periodic waves, closed-domain conservation, local dispersion, and GeoClaw comparisons. GeoClaw uses three fixed benchmark cases at $192$ and $384$ computational grids; the earlier normalized-domain ablation has a different construction and is reported separately.

\paragraph{Matched refinement against GeoClaw.}
We compare the newly computed shallow-water trajectories at $128$- and $256$-cell cores against GeoClaw on both $192$- and $384$-cell computational grids for the three paired cases. Initial bathymetry, depth, zero momentum, physical domain, requested times, and spatial restriction are checked at each grid level. This gives $24$ comparisons on the common $50 \times 64 \times 64$ elevation arrays. GeoClaw retains open extrapolation boundaries while the in-house solvers retain radiation boundaries and the sponge; the comparison therefore measures inter-code agreement under these different boundary treatments. Holding the GeoClaw grid fixed isolates the measured effect of refining the in-house computation, without identifying its error against an exact solution.

\paragraph{Full-horizon numerical sensitivity.}
For the two shallow-water references, we fix six test cases before inspecting their new numerical sensitivities: the first test scenario in each of the continental/Gaussian, trench/rough, island/multi-Gaussian, seamounts/Okada-like, canyon/dipole, and seamounts/fault combinations. This covers all five bathymetry families and six source families, with one case for each selected pair rather than a population-representative sample. Each case is advanced to the same 50 requested times through $420$ on solver cores of $64$, $128$, and $256$ cells per axis, corresponding to $96$, $192$, and $384$ buffered computational grids. The physical core and buffer extents remain fixed. The $128$-cell initial bathymetry and conserved depth fields are held as raster cell averages: coarser inputs use area means, while finer inputs repeat each parent cell average. Requested states are retained in double precision, and elevation fields are restricted to the shared $64 \times 64$ core for comparison. The differences therefore describe combined space--time numerical sensitivity of fixed raster inputs, rather than a formal convergence order for a smooth underlying seabed.

At the $128$-cell core, both solvers are also evaluated at target CFL $0.1125$ and half that value. Common target CFL does not imply identical natural time steps, and halving it changes both time integration and interpolation to the requested outputs. Boundary controls use the same three cases as the GeoClaw comparison. We separately remove the sponge, replace radiation with open boundaries on the unchanged no-sponge domain, and double the buffer width with radiation boundaries and no sponge. The enlarged domain extends bathymetry and initial depth by edge replication, so that contrast includes the assumed exterior state. We record trajectory and per-time RMSE, minimum depth, velocity, and numerical-state corrections, together with water volume and mechanical energy per unit density over the full computational domain. Changes in integrated volume and mechanical energy are measured relative to each computation's initial state; open boundaries and damping prevent interpreting these changes as closed-system conservation tests.

Stationary and moving equilibria provide complementary source--flux tests. Five lake-at-rest cases use one representative of each bathymetry family and zero initial elevation and momentum, retaining the benchmark grid and full horizon. For moving flow, a smooth periodic bed $b(x) = -2 + 0.2 \cos(2\pi x/2400)$ carries constant discharge $hu = 0.2$, $hv = 0$. Initial depth is obtained from the subcritical branch of $h + b + (hu) ^ 2 / (2gh ^ 2)=0$. Both solvers are advanced to $420$ on $128 \times 8$ and $256 \times 8$ grids with periodic boundaries and no sponge. The flow is one-dimensional, represented by eight identical transverse cells at each longitudinal resolution. Elevation and discharge drift are measured against the sampled steady solution. Nonzero drift is retained in the results; the test does not assume exact moving-equilibrium preservation.

\paragraph{Fixed-prediction sensitivity to reference resolution.}
On the same six cases, we evaluate FNO, F-FNO, U-FNO, and ConvLSTM checkpoints trained with seeds $18$, $36$, and $67$. Each checkpoint receives its original $64 \times 64$ inputs and produces one unchanged prediction, restored with its training normalization. That prediction is scored against the archived Hydrostatic target and the newly computed $64$-, $128$-, and $256$-cell-core targets, all restricted to the same $64 \times 64$ output. We report physical RMSE and mean per-scenario relative $L_2$, average the latter across the three seeds, and compare model orderings within each case and seed. This is a descriptive test of changing the evaluation reference, rather than retraining or native neural-network resolution transfer.

The $92$-rollout plan combines the six-case grid/CFL study, three-case boundary controls, five bathymetry families for stationary tests, and two periodic moving-flow resolutions. Dataset checks verify the $384\rightarrow128\rightarrow192\rightarrow64$ construction, split-qualified identities, shared inputs, output coverage, and numerical criteria. Separate training-pool sensitivity cases assess CFL, time extraction, and repeatability.

The GeoClaw comparisons are descriptive rather than formal convergence studies. The current comparator refinement retains the physical domain and raster inputs while changing the external grid and adaptive time steps; the archived in-house fields remain fixed in that comparison. Boundary and algorithmic differences persist between codes. These analyses do not establish observed-event validity, shoreline accuracy, generally non-reflecting boundaries, formal convergence, or equivalence with an operational tsunami code.

Each dataset trajectory is additionally screened for finiteness, positivity/depth, amplitude, velocity, sanitization, projection, clipping, post-step CFL, and, where relevant, conjugate-gradient convergence. Trajectories that do not satisfy these criteria are excluded.

\subsection{Field and physics metrics}
\label{subsec:eval-metrics}
Field errors are evaluated elementwise across entire trajectories using standard metrics:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_k | \widehat{y}_k - y_k |,
\qquad
\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_k(\widehat{y}_k - y_k) ^ 2},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{rel}\, L_2 = \frac{\|\widehat{y} - y \|_2}{\| y \|_2},
\qquad
\mathrm{max\text{-}err}=\max_k | \widehat{y}_k - y_k |.
\end{equation}
where $k$ indexes all space-time grid points. MAE, RMSE, and relative $L_2$ are computed in denormalized benchmark-scale units. Maximum absolute error ($\mathrm{max\text{-}err}$) is reported in normalized target units unless explicitly noted otherwise.

Full-split metrics accumulate sums before reduction, yielding global relative $L_2$ and maximum error. Subgroups average per-scenario metrics; the two aggregations are labelled separately.

For the selected single-seed Hydrostatic architecture comparison, we also use a paired nonparametric bootstrap: one shared index draw resamples the same $2{,}500$ scenario identities $2{,}000$ times with seed $20260813$. We report mean per-scenario denormalized relative $L_2$ and paired differences from FNO. These are test-scenario intervals for fixed checkpoints, not training-seed uncertainty or population-level architecture rankings.

Ensemble seeds $\{11, 22, 33, 44, 55, 66, 77\}$ assess FNO seed stability, not replicated architecture comparisons.

Per-frame relative $L_2$ errors evaluate spatial slices at specific time instances. Physical conservation properties are monitored via domain-averaged quantities:
\begin{equation}
\overline{\eta}(t) = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{i, j}\eta_{i, j}(t),
\qquad
\overline{h}(t) = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{i, j}\bigl(\eta_{i, j}(t) - b_{i, j}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\overline{h}$ is a fully wet mass proxy rather than an independently conserved model output. Because the bathymetry is fixed, the numerator of this proxy is exactly the spatial-mean free-surface error, while its relative denominator includes the background water column. A small relative mass-proxy value therefore measures agreement in depth mean and must not be interpreted as evidence of conservation. Spectral error is computed from orthonormal two-dimensional FFT coefficients in radial wavenumber bands $\kappa \leq 8$, $8 < \kappa \leq 16$, and $\kappa > 16$:
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathcal{B}} = \frac{\|\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{F}(\widehat{Y} - Y)\|_2}{\|\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{F}(Y)\|_2}.
\end{equation}
These diagnostics localize error in integral and spectral structure; none of the neural models enforces a conservation law.

\subsection{Generalization and resolution transfer}
\label{subsec:generalization-protocol}

Metadata-filtered \texttt{multi-gauss}, \texttt{trench}, and source-strength groups remain within training support. Strict tests instead remove a family before training and fit normalization on the retained training set. Cross-dataset inference restores inputs with dataset statistics, re-standardizes them with checkpoint-training statistics, and evaluates denormalized predictions and targets in one benchmark space.

The proxy resolution diagnostic resizes the processed Hydrostatic test fields to $32 \times 32$ or $128 \times 128$ and applies the same $64 \times 64$ checkpoint. The native experiment instead uses paired MUSCL-HR simulations at $32$, $64$, and $128$ resolution. All three resolutions start from a shared $384 \times 384$ master input and use the same 50 requested times from $8.4$ to $420$. Three checkpoints are trained separately, and the full train-grid/test-grid matrix is evaluated. Each target dataset is rebased into the checkpoint's training normalization before inference. The reported values come from the September 2026 evaluation, which included all nine train-grid/test-grid combinations.

\subsection{Runtime benchmarking}
\label{subsec:runtime}

To compare computational cost, we measure the repeated forward task under fixed hardware and precision. The numerical references are timed in float64 on the local CPU using the requested-time $192 \times 192$ rollout with the central $128 \times 128$ region reduced by cell averaging to $64 \times 64$, excluding bathymetry and source generation. The reference solvers use vectorized NumPy/SciPy implementations; each timing uses eight fixed scenarios, one warm-up pass, and three measured repeats. Direct surrogates are timed on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4050 Laptop GPU with an Intel Core i5-13420H CPU, in fp32 at batch size one, with TF32 disabled, five warm-up passes, twenty measured passes, and CUDA synchronization. The reported ratio is $T_{\mathrm{reference}}/T_{\mathrm{surrogate}}$, using mean rollout time per sample.

Loading, preparation, and solver construction are excluded from the timing ratio. These hardware-specific forward costs do not establish end-to-end latency or speedups over compiled, GPU, or adaptive-mesh solvers.

\subsection{Cross-reference discrepancy}
\label{subsec:cross-solver}

The raw solver gap compares two numerical references on the same scenarios and requested times. A second diagnostic compares a surrogate trained on reference $A$ with reference $B$:
\begin{equation}
\rho(A \rightarrow B) = 
\frac{\mathrm{RMSE}(f_\theta ^ A, \mathrm{ref}\, B)}
     {\mathrm{RMSE}(\mathrm{ref}\, A, \mathrm{ref}\, B)}.
\end{equation}
All terms use the global denormalized benchmark-scale RMSE, and confidence intervals use 2,000 scenario bootstrap resamples. A value $\rho < 1$ means that the surrogate is closer to reference $B$ in this benchmark metric than reference $A$ is. Without observational truth or a stronger physical reference, this is not evidence of physical superiority.

For the squared-error interpretation, let $d_{AB} = Y_A - Y_B$ be the raw reference difference and $e_A = \widehat{Y}_A - Y_A$ be the surrogate error relative to its training reference, with all fields vectorized over the same scenarios, times, and spatial cells. The cross-reference numerator then obeys the exact identity
\begin{equation}
\rho(A \rightarrow B)^2
= 1 + \frac{\lVert e_A \rVert_2 ^ 2}{\lVert d_{AB} \rVert_2 ^ 2}
+ 2\frac{\langle e_A,d_{AB}\rangle}{\lVert d_{AB} \rVert_2 ^ 2}.
\end{equation}
The first term after one is the surrogate-error contribution and is nonnegative; the alignment term can either increase or reduce the ratio. This decomposition describes the benchmark metric exactly, but it does not identify which reference is closer to physical truth.

\subsection{Pooled-reference experiment}
\label{subsec:pooled-reference}
To test whether one surrogate averages across numerical references, we combine matched Hydrostatic, MUSCL-HR, and Boussinesq training trajectories into a shared training set. The solver-anonymous FNO receives the three spatial input fields used throughout the study and is trained without solver identity. The solver-conditioned FNO receives one additional channel encoding the requested reference, allowing the same model to represent separate solver-specific mappings. Training batches are balanced across the three references, and both variants use the same architecture and optimization objective.

The primary comparison uses matched training-update budgets and three independent seeds, $\{18, 36, 67\}$. A secondary comparison permits approximately three times as many pooled examples per epoch; it is reported separately because it changes the amount of training exposure as well as the optimization schedule. All comparisons use the same $2{,}500$ paired held-out scenarios. Before paired evaluation, each input channel is restored using the statistics of its source dataset; the initial-depth input remains in its stored benchmark scale because it is not standardized.

\subsection{Gauge, arrival, and peak diagnostics}
\label{subsec:wave-metrics}

We evaluate nine fixed virtual gauges arranged on a $3 \times 3$ interior grid. For target waveform $y_g(t_k)$ and prediction $\widehat{y}_g(t_k)$, the waveform normalized RMSE is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NRMSE}_g = \frac{\sqrt{T ^ {-1} \sum_k \bigl(\widehat{y}_g(t_k) - y_g(t_k) \bigr) ^ 2}}{\max_k | y_g(t_k) |}.
\end{equation}

Gauges with target amplitude at or below $10 ^ {-12}$ are excluded from waveform NRMSE and relative-peak summaries and counted as inactive, avoiding division by a numerically zero target. The gauge-arrival threshold is $10\%$ of the target gauge's maximum absolute amplitude and is never changed by the prediction. A target gauge that never crosses the threshold is inactive; one already active at $t_1$ is excluded because the true arrival occurred before the observed window. An eligible gauge is compared only if the prediction also crosses the threshold, and missing predicted arrivals are counted separately. Peak elevation is the maximum signed surface value. To reduce one-frame instability in the time-to-peak metric, all times within $1\%$ of the target or predicted peak-to-trough range are treated as a plateau and averaged.

A separate arrival-map diagnostic uses a single target-only threshold equal to $10\%$ of the sample-wide maximum absolute target elevation, applies it at every pixel, and reports requested-frame index differences only where both prediction and target cross. Its eligibility fraction is reported together with the error. The summary first computes mean absolute index error over the eligible pixels of each scenario and then averages those scenario values; it does not pool all eligible pixels into one global reduction. These are normalized-domain virtual-observation diagnostics, not comparisons with measured coastal gauges.

\subsection{Uncertainty quantification}
\label{subsec:uncertainty}
A seven-member FNO deep ensemble differs only in seed \citep{lakshminarayanan2017deepensembles}. For member predictions $\widehat{Y} ^ {(m)}$, the ensemble mean $\bar{Y}$ and variance $\sigma^2$ are computed pointwise via:
\begin{equation}
\bar{Y} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m = 1} ^ {M}\widehat{Y} ^ {(m)},
\qquad
\sigma ^ 2 = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m = 1} ^ {M}\left(\widehat{Y} ^ {(m)} - \bar{Y}\right) ^ 2.
\end{equation}
Under a Gaussian error assumption, the central prediction interval corresponding to a nominal coverage probability $p$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
\bar{Y}\pm z_p\sigma,
\qquad
z_p = \sqrt{2}\, \operatorname{erf} ^ {-1}(p).
\end{equation}
We report the empirical coverage and the Pearson correlation coefficient between $\sigma$ and $|\bar{Y} - Y|$. As a study-defined interval-scale correction for raw ensemble under-dispersion, a single scalar variance scaling factor $\gamma$ is estimated using only the validation subset:
\begin{equation}
\gamma = \max\left\{1,\, \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{val}}} \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{val}}} \frac{(Y_k - \bar{Y}_k)^2}{\max(\sigma_k^2, \varepsilon)}\right\}.
\end{equation}
The calibrated predictive interval is obtained by rescaling $\sigma$ to $\sqrt{\gamma}\sigma$, applying this factor directly to both the test and subgroup evaluation sets without further tuning. Because the empirical coverage is computed marginally across correlated pixels and frames, these metrics serve as interval-scale reliability diagnostics rather than calibrated event probabilities.

We additionally evaluate simultaneous coverage of an entire requested trajectory. For each of the $1{,}000$ validation scenarios, we compute
\begin{equation}
S_i = \max_{t, x, y} \frac{|Y_i (t, x, y) - \bar{Y}_i(t, x, y)|}{\max\{\sigma_i(t, x, y), \epsilon_\sigma\}},
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon_\sigma$ is $10 ^ {-6}$ times the training target standard deviation. For nominal coverage $p\in\{0.50, 0.80, 0.90, 0.95\}$, the multiplier $q_p$ is the validation score at ordered rank $\lceil(1{,}000 + 1)p\rceil$. The resulting band is $\bar{Y}\pm q_p\max\{\sigma, \epsilon_\sigma\}$. A test scenario is covered only when every cell at all 50 requested times lies inside this band. The validation multipliers are applied unchanged to all $2{,}500$ test scenarios and to their source, bathymetry, and high-strength subgroups; the latter uses the same source-strength threshold, $s\geq0.285$, as the marginal-coverage analysis. We also rank test scenarios by their mean predicted standard deviation and report error and coverage as increasingly uncertain scenarios are removed. This is a retrospective ranking within the test cohort, rather than a deployment threshold. Because validation also informed checkpoint selection, these are empirical reliability measurements, without an independent-calibration coverage guarantee.
