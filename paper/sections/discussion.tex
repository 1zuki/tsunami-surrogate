\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Main findings}
The central result is that the numerical reference and its output contract can contribute an error scale comparable to, or larger than, the surrogate error being reported. The common-time construction does not change the basic machine-learning task, but it gives every label one shared temporal meaning and prevents solver-specific natural step counts from defining incompatible targets. This reference-aware separation is a methodological control rather than an architecture contribution, yet it materially changes which scientific claims the benchmark can support.

The established neural architectures serve as controlled demonstrations of that result. Among the selected single-seed Hydrostatic configurations, F-FNO has the lowest error and ConvLSTM is a close second, but the architecture comparison is a speed--accuracy--conditioning trade-off rather than one universal or seed-replicated ranking. The seeded window models further reduce trajectory error only after receiving the first requested target frame, and ConvLSTM's recurrent improvement comes with substantially higher inference time.

\subsection{Effect of reference choice}
Once the outputs are compared at identical requested times, Hydrostatic and MUSCL-HR remain the closest pair. Their global RMSE gap, $0.00508$, is nevertheless about $1.6$ times the same-reference Hydrostatic FNO RMSE and about $2.1$ times the F-FNO RMSE. Thus even within the shallow-water family, the choice of numerical reference is not a small perturbation relative to the model error. The two shallow-water--Boussinesq gaps are much larger still: their RMSE values near $0.045$ are more than five times the Boussinesq FNO's same-reference RMSE.

The cross-reference discrepancy ratios reflect these scales. Both shallow-water directions remain above one, so neither FNO prediction bridges the Hydrostatic--MUSCL-HR gap. Several cross-family directions lie only slightly below one, and one confidence interval reaches one. When two references are already far apart, smoothing or bias in a surrogate can move its output marginally closer to the other reference in a global norm. This is not evidence that a network corrects its training solver toward physical truth. Without observations or a demonstrably stronger reference, ``closer to another solver'' and ``more physically accurate'' remain different statements.

The qualified GeoClaw comparison reinforces this caution. Analytical and refinement checks support the implemented regimes, but the exploratory production-like fields are not pointwise equivalent to GeoClaw. The retained compatibility canaries compare the $96 \times 96$ radiation-plus-sponge in-house domain with a $192 \times 192$ open, no-sponge GeoClaw domain before taking the same central $64 \times 64$ crop. Their discrepancies can additionally combine differences in reconstruction order, flux and topographic-source treatment, time integration, and initial-state embedding outside the crop. The present comparison does not isolate those factors, so we do not assign the disagreement to any one cause. Instead, the matched-regime checks support the finite-horizon implementations, while the broader disagreement limits transfer of conclusions between numerical references. The reported surrogate errors should therefore be read as emulation errors against explicit in-house references, not as errors against an externally certified solution.

\subsection{Model behavior}
The direct ranking is broadly consistent with the inductive-bias argument of Section~\ref{sec:models}, but it does not reduce to ``spectral is always better.'' F-FNO obtains the lowest direct Hydrostatic error with far fewer parameters than FNO, while ConvLSTM performs strongly through explicit temporal feedback. Axis-wise factorization may act as both a parameter reduction and a useful structural regularizer, but this mechanism is not isolated by the present experiment.

Increasing the retained FNO modes from $14$ to $20$ does not improve the result, despite approximately doubling the parameter count, and the eight-mode ablation is slightly better than the base model in this seed. More spectral bandwidth is therefore not the limiting factor by itself. Possible explanations include finite data, optimization, low-frequency dominance of the targets, or the pointwise head and padding treatment. A dedicated mode/depth factorial study would be needed to distinguish them.

The per-frame and spectral diagnostics provide a more specific view. ConvLSTM's late-time error is lower than the direct FNO's, consistent with the benefit of recurrent state propagation, but its high-wavenumber error is larger. F-FNO improves low-frequency and integral behavior while making only a small change in the high-frequency band. Rough sources remain difficult in both subgroup and strict-holdout tests. The present evidence does not determine whether that difficulty comes from spectral truncation, the rough-family training density, source amplitude semantics, or their interaction.

\subsection{Boussinesq comparison reference}
The Boussinesq FNO has the lowest same-reference relative $L_2$ among the three FNO targets and an almost flat per-frame curve, but its denormalized benchmark-scale RMSE is the largest. These two facts are compatible because relative error divides by the target norm, which differs across references. Gauge diagnostics also show a much larger waveform-error tail and a very small arrival-eligible subset. The low arrival-map error cannot be interpreted as a superior arrival model because only about $21\%$ of pixels are eligible and most virtual gauges are already active at the first requested time.

This reference is therefore useful as a deliberately different weakly dispersive label family, not as an automatic fidelity upgrade. The constant-depth dispersion diagnostic confirms the implemented discrete trend, and the independent comparison supports selected small-amplitude fully wet regimes. The in-house Boussinesq solver nevertheless remains an elevation-only linear model rather than a full nonlinear coastal solver.

\subsection{Generalization, resolution, and morphology transfer}
Generalization is mixed and depends strongly on what is held out. Metadata subgroups remain within the original training support and show which portions of the test distribution are difficult. Strict family removal changes both the examples seen during optimization and the fitted normalization statistics; the large continental and rough-source errors are consequently stronger evidence of extrapolation difficulty, although they do not identify which of those two changes is responsible.

The two resolution studies answer different questions. Proxy resizing asks whether a $64 \times 64$ checkpoint tolerates resampled arrays. The native MUSCL-HR matrix asks whether separately simulated fields and separately trained checkpoints transfer across grids after an explicit normalization bridge. Both degrade away from the training setup, but the native diagonal itself also worsens with resolution while each model sees only $700$ training scenarios. The results therefore do not establish a clean resolution-scaling law or arbitrary mesh invariance.

The real-bathymetry morphology suite is more encouraging: direct F-FNO transfers with lower error than direct FNO on the ten fully wet crops. Its scope is still narrow. Depths are rescaled into the training range, forcing is synthetic, and no observed event is reconstructed. The near-unity coastline stress errors show that useful offshore morphology transfer does not extend to positive topography, shoreline motion, or wet--dry inundation.

\subsection{Uncertainty interpretation}
The seven-member ensemble provides two distinct observations. First, seed-to-seed mean error is fairly stable for this Hydrostatic FNO configuration. Second, raw member spread is too small: empirical coverage falls below nominal across the reliability curve. The validation-only scalar inflation corrects much of the average scale but cannot change the spatial ranking of uncertainty, model multimodality, or subgroup-specific shape.

Marginal coverage also counts hundreds of millions of correlated pixel--time elements, not independent events. A test coverage near $90\%$ after scaling therefore means that the interval width is approximately right on average under this elementwise convention. It does not mean that $90\%$ of complete tsunami scenarios, coastlines, or hazards are contained. The high-strength subgroup remains under-covered after scaling, demonstrating that one global factor is not a substitute for distribution-aware calibration.

\subsection{Practical implications}
The benchmark is a controlled study of surrogate behavior, not an operational tsunami forecasting tool or site-specific hazard assessment. Its strongest practical result is narrower: once a numerical reference and data contract are fixed, several models emulate a 50-frame trajectory in milliseconds and retain useful accuracy on fully wet unseen morphologies. This can support rapid scenario screening, sensitivity studies, or methodological experiments in which the reference solver remains explicit.

Moving beyond that role requires more than a faster network. A deployable system would need physical coordinates and units, realistic source models, surveyed bathymetry, friction and wet--dry physics, validated boundaries, observed gauges, operational solver comparisons, and event-level uncertainty assessment. The current speed, transfer, and ensemble results are ingredients for that longer program, not evidence that it has already been completed.
